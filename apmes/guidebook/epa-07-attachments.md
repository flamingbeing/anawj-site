### Trouble shooting inadequate or failed labour epidural/CSE - with DOPs

A term nulliparous woman presented at 3–4 cm cervical dilation in labour with irregular contractions.
A combined spinal epidural was established with intrathecal administration of 2mg ropivacaine 0.1% and fentanyl 15 mcg diluted with saline to a total of 2 mls and maintenance using patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA) with low- dose mixture ropivacaine 0.1% + fentanyl 2 mcg/ml of background infusion of 8-mls/h and 5-mls boluses available every 15 minutes as required.
Four hours later, the patient complained of numbness on her left side and increasing discomfort on her right side. On physical examination, she was unable to move her left leg but had complete range of motion of her right leg.

**Entrustment question 1.** What would you do in the initial management of the breakthrough pain?

**Entrustment discussions:**

Breakthrough pain should be managed in a logical, stepwise workflow to promptly diagnose the cause and institute appropriate treatment.

Systematic approach should be taken to improve her analgesia:

1. Assess the patient:
   - Severity of pain - pain score
   - Nature – pressure vs sharp
   - Site of pain: identify occipito posterior pains
   - Rate of labour progression – stage of cervical dilatation, augmentation with oxytocin
2. Assess the analgesia:
   - Use of PCEA - demands requested vs doses delivered
   - Assess the block – sensory dermatomal level in caudad and cephalad direction, symmetry, and motor block (bromage score)
3. Check equipment:
   - Visual inspection of catheter including the “alligator” clip
   - Check entry site against documented procedure
4. Determine the issues and top up the epidural accordingly
5. Prepare top up solution according to institution guideline and practice

If there is no demonstrable anaesthetic level after an appropriate dose, the epidural catheter should be replaced at this time.
If 2 or more breakthrough pains, to inform specialist on call and counsel for possible re-siting of epidural.

**Entrustment question 2.** Explain the rationale in your initial management for the breakthrough pain

**Entrustment discussions:**

During the first stage of labour, a T10–L1 level is necessary for adequate analgesia, and during the second stage of labour, the patient will need an anaesthetic level extending down to S2–S4.

**Pain despite adequate block level:**

Likely causes:

- Insufficient block density
- Localized pain at perineum/back eg. occiput-posterior foetal position
- Consider obstetric causes

Treatment options:

- Increase block density with supplementation of higher concentration of local anaesthetic solution
- Addition of opioid (eg. fentanyl) or adjuncts (eg. clonidine)

**Bilateral inadequate block extent**

Likely causes:

- Insufficient epidural drug volume/spread
  - Continuous infusion: inadequate infusion rate
  - Intermittent bolus: inadequate bolus volume or extended interval between boluses
- Mal-positioned multi-orifice catheter (one or more orifices positioned out of epidural space)
- Wrong drug in the bag/ no LA in bag
- Technical errors or pump failure

Treatment options:

- Supplementation with higher volume dilute local anaesthetic solution to improve spread

**Unilateral block or missed segments**

Likely causes:

- Paramedian catheter position
- Anatomical barriers, eg. epidural septa
- Lateral patient position resulting in epidural drug spread to dependent side

Treatment options:

- Withdrawing catheter slightly prior to supplementation
- Supplementation with higher volume dilute local anaesthetic solution to improve spread
- Missed segment (patchy block) might need to be treated with by increasing block density with supplementation of higher concentration of local anaesthetic solution

**No sensory blockade**

Likely causes:

- Drug delivery failure (pump failure, disconnection, kinked catheter, etc.)
- Catheter is not positioned in epidural space

Treatment options:

- Careful aspiration to confirm catheter is not intravascular, before administering a test dose
- Supplementation with dilute local anaesthetic solution if confirmed to be in the epidural space

Failure of the above measures to re-establish analgesia should prompt the provider to exclude serious obstetric causes, and to replace the epidural catheter (preferably via CSE).

**Entrustment question 3.** What are the predictors and consequences of suboptimal labour analgesia?

**Entrustment discussions:**

Factors Associated with Inadequate Labour Epidural Analgesia:

- Advanced age
- Morbid obesity
- Scoliosis
- Spinal disc surgery
- Multiparity
- Cervical dilatation > 7 cm

Consequences of suboptimal analgesia:

- Inadequate labour analgesia leading to patient's dissatisfaction
- Epidural inadvertently in wrong spaces including
  - subarachnoid and subdural leading to high block
  - Intravascular leading to LAST
- Multiple breakthrough pain has been shown to be associated with failed extended epidural anaesthesia resulting in conversion to GA for operative delivery

**Entrustment question 4.** How would you optimise labour epidural analgesia in the morbidly obese patient?

**Entrustment discussions:**

1. Electively placing a functional epidural catheter early is advantageous to avoid any time delay because of difficult insertion -
   - Allow confirmation of optimal analgesia during early labour
     - Allow sufficient time to manage a failed epidural
     - Ability to extend for epidural anaesthesia should any emergency operative intervention be required (avoiding GA in such cases)
2. Key to successful epidural placement depends on midline identification
   - Optimize positioning - sitting often allows for easier midline identification
   - Ultrasound assisted neuraxial block may reduce number of insertion attempts
3. CSE technique is associated with both higher initial analgesia and less frequent failure when compared with plain epidural
4. Loss of resistance to saline was more superior to air with regards to ultimate success of block as well as fewer complications
5. An appropriate length of epidural catheter should be left in the epidural space in anticipation of high incidence of migration of the epidural catheter in fatty subcutaneous tissues leading to failed epidural

### Managing a High/Total Spinal

1 hour later, you received a call from the midwife informing of a high block and patient has difficulty with breathing.

**Entrustment question 1.** What are the situations that can give rise to unexpected high block?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

1. Unintentional or unrecognized injection of LA (via a needle or catheter) into either the subarachnoid or the subdural space during the planned initiation of epidural analgesia
2. The epidural catheter may migrate into the subarachnoid or subdural space during the course of labour and delivery
3. Overdose of LA either through prolonged duration of infusion or multiple boluses in the epidural space

**Entrustment question 2.** How would you manage a high/total spinal block

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Early recognition - agitation, dyspnoea, difficulty in speaking and profound hypotension may herald the onset of total spinal
- Avoid aortocaval compression
- Administer 100% oxygen
- Provide PPV, preferably through PPV
- Monitor maternal vital signs, ECG, and foetal heart rate
- Support maternal circulation with IV fluids and vasopressors as needed. Do not hesitate to give adrenaline if needed
- Maintain verbal communication with the mother or administer a sedative-hypnotic (after treating any hypotension and hypoxemia) because total spinal anaesthesia does not signal brain anaesthesia. Patients may lose consciousness and stop breathing because of central nervous system hypoperfusion, not brain anaesthesia.

**Entrustment question 3.** What can be done to prevent high/spinal block?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Aspiration - look out for CSF but not a completely reliable on its own
- Other methods of confirming that it’s not CSF (glucose POCT)
- Administer appropriate dose - lignocaine 30 - 45 mg and careful assessment of spinal block

**Entrustment question 4.** How would you diagnose a subdural block and what are the management?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

A subdural injection may be difficult to diagnose because onset is later than that with an intrathecal injection and more closely resembles that associated with epidural neuroblockade. Subdural injection of local anaesthetic typically results in unexpectedly high (but patchy) blockade with an onset time that is intermediate between that of spinal and epidural anaesthesia (i.e., 10 to 20 minutes). Cranial spread is more extensive than caudal spread of the local anaesthetic, so sacral analgesia typically is absent. The block may involve the cranial nerves. (The subdural space, unlike the epidural space, extends intracranially.) Thus, apnoea and unconsciousness can occur during a subdural block. Horner's syndrome has been reported. A subdural block usually results in less intense motor blockade than the blockade that occurs with high or total spinal anaesthesia. This difference may reflect the limited spread of the local anaesthetic within the subdural space, which helps spare the anterior motor fibres. Subdural block results in less severe hypotension than that with high or total spinal anaesthesia, most likely because subdural injection leads to less sympathetic blockade than spinal anaesthesia. The unpredictable spread of local anaesthetic, the slower onset of maximal spread (in comparison with spinal anaesthesia), the patchy nature of the block, and the sacral sparing make it difficult to use a subdural catheter safely during labour and delivery. If it is suspected that a catheter is positioned within the subdural space, it should be replaced with an epidural catheter.

### Managing foetal distress/non-reassuring foetal status post RA

Labour progress was slow, and an oxytocin infusion was started. On re-examination, the patient’s cervix was found to be 6 cm dilated. The cardiotocograph (CTG) became nonreassuring, demonstrating late decelerations for 15 minutes occurring with over 50 percent of contractions. A decision was made to perform a foetal scalp blood sample, but while sampling in the supine position a prolonged deceleration to 65 beats/min occurred. The obstetrician ordered a Category I emergency caesarean delivery (immediate threat to life of woman or foetus) with a maximum intended decision-delivery interval of 30 minutes.

**Entrustment question 1.** What are the immediate measures you can perform as intrauterine resuscitation?

**Entrustment discussions:**

- Intrauterine resuscitation should be instituted :
  - Administer high flow oxygen via NRM
  - Stop syntocinon infusion (if it is being used)
  - Position patient to full left lateral (guided by FHR); continue for transfer and on operating table [if fetal heart rate remains severely abnormal try right lateral/knee- elbow positions in case of umbilical cord compression]
  - Administer tocolytic
  - Rapid IV fluid infusion of 500 - 1000 mls crystalloid solution
  - Administer vasopressors (ephedrine) if low maternal blood pressure
- Reassess if intrauterine resuscitation measures lead to partial or complete recovery of fetal heart decelerations or bradycardia

**Entrustment question 2.** Why and when are these measures considered appropriate?

**Entrustment discussions:**

Acute fetal distress in labour is a condition of progressive fetal asphyxia with hypoxia and acidosis. It is usually diagnosed by finding characteristic features in the fetal heart rate pattern, wherever possible supported by fetal scalp pH measurement.
Intrauterine resuscitation consists of applying specific measures with the aim of increasing oxygen delivery to the placenta and umbilical blood flow, in order to reverse hypoxia and acidosis. Intrauterine resuscitation may be used as part of the obstetric management of labour, while preparing for caesarean delivery for fetal distress, or at the time of establishment of regional analgesia during labour in the compromised fetus.
Treatment to reduce or halt uterine activity will improve fetal distress caused both by reduced oxygen supply to the placenta and by most umbilical cord compression.
Active tocolysis is much more effective than stopping the oxytocin infusion during augmented labour.
Maternal oxygen administration during fetal distress increases fetal oxygenation.
The left lateral position is used to improve abnormal FHR exacerbated by aortocaval compression. However, in some cases, the abnormality may be caused by umbilical Cord compression which is unrelieved or made worse in the left lateral position. Further position change must then be tried, to right lateral or knee- elbow (prone crouch with knees & elbows on the bed).

**Entrustment question 3.** How and where will you top up the epidural for surgical anaesthesia?

**Entrustment discussions:**

- Rapid assessment of the adequacy of epidural dermatomal level to determine its suitability and safety for surgical anaesthesia.
- Ideally assessment of whether an epidural is likely to provide effective analgesia for the duration of labour and anaesthesia for surgery should be made early and be continued during labour.
- Good communication with the attending midwife is important to monitor the effectiveness of analgesia and to identify any problems early - should have a low threshold to resite if an epidural is functioning poorly

<u>Safety assessment and rapid epidural top-up for Cat 1 caesarean delivery</u>

**Safety assessment (15 secs)**

- Check vital maternal vital signs - HR, BP
- Baseline sensory block level and bromage score
- Documented breakthrough pain and top ups

**Prepare 20 mls of top-up mixture** containing 17 ml lignocaine 2% + 1 ml 1:10,000 adrenaline + 2 ml 8.4% preservative free sodium bicarbonate (to be prepared by a second anaesthetist)

**Perform aspiration test**

- After negative aspiration test, a fractionated epidural top-up should be administered

**Test dose (90 sec)**

- Give 3-ml test and wait for 30 sec - assess for signs of spinal block (high sensory block, motor block and hypotension)
- Give further 3-ml and wait for 60 secs - observe for signs of IV placement after 30 seconds (tinnitus, perioral numbness, drowsiness)

**Main dose (90 sec)**

- Give remainder while observing for any changes as above
- Stay with patient and maintain communication at all times. Hand on pulse and monitor BP. Be prepared to deal with high block

**Entrustment question 4.** What circumstances (include classification for caesarean section urgency) will you not use epidural top for surgical anaesthesia?

**Entrustment discussions:**

- Category 1 such as persistent bradycardia unresponsive to intrauterine resuscitation or cord prolapse
  - There are four-category urgency classification for caesarean delivery

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| I * | Immediate threat to the life of woman or fetus |
| 2 | Maternal or fetal compromise that is not immediate life-threatening |
| 3 | No maternal or fetal compromise but needs early delivery |
| 4 | Elective - delivery timed to suit woman or staff |

- Multiple breakthrough pain requiring very frequent epidural top-ups may indicate a poorly functioning epidural - with 2 provisos:
  - Women who are highly anxious with expectation or wish to have no sensation at all evidenced by frequent self-administered PCEA boluses - documented low pain scores with no breakthrough pain
  - Obstructed labour (dystocia) will present with more pain than normal labour - hence breakthrough pain may occur even with a good epidural

### Managing Inadvertent Dural Puncture

You are administering an epidural for labour analgesia in a 25-year-old primigravida, with a 16G Tuohy needle when a wet tap occurs

**Entrustment question 1.** What is your immediate course of management?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Re-do the epidural: remove the Tuohy needle and reinsert it at an adjacent space
- Intrathecal catheter insertion: insert the epidural catheter into the subarachnoid space
- General: Document clearly, explain and inform patient and managing team

**Entrustment question 2.** State the advantages and disadvantages of both options and how do you decide one over the other?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

| | Advantages | Disadvantages. |
|---|---|---|
| Spinal | Rapid and predictable analgesia<br>Prevent another inadvertent puncture | Risk of infection<br>Cauda equine syndrome |
| Resite | | Potential risk of another dural puncture.<br>May not be able to get epidural space esp if it has been difficult with initial multiple attempts.<br>Drug dosing unclear. |

In cases of known dural punctures, the immediate management of the patient depends on a number of different factors including the experience of the anaesthetist, the staffing on labour ward and the status of the patient.

**Entrustment question 3.** What special precautions would you take if you had inserted a spinal catheter?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- The epidural catheter can be inserted into the intrathecal space and top-ups administered by an anaesthetist only, with no infusions attached.
- The catheter must be very clearly labelled and handed over to the senior midwifery and anaesthetic teams.
- Regular neurological observations to be done
- Expect tachyphylaxis as labour progresses. This technique is likely to achieve good analgesia initially, but there is an increased risk of analgesic failure because of a lack of familiarity among the anaesthetic and midwifery staff, and the catheter may also be pulled out of the intrathecal space.
- Aseptic precaution while handling the intrathecal catheter during top ups

**Entrustment question 4.** What would you give to initiate the analgesia if you were inserting a spinal catheter and how would you maintain the labour analgesia?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Initiation with 2 mg ropivacaine + 15 mcg Fentanyl through the spinal catheter
- Maintenance: by infusion 1 ml/hr of ropivacaine 0.1% with fentanyl 2 mcg/ml (usually 0 – 2 ls/hr)

**Entrustment question 5.** How would you top up the spinal catheter in the event where patient need operative delivery?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Prior discussion with the anaesthetic specialist/consultant before top up
- Check the block height before top up – as baseline
- Administer 0.5 to 1 ml doses of heavy bupivacaine 0.5% + up to a total of 15 mcg of fentanyl
- Use 3 mls syringe for accurate dosing
- Monitor block height to determine whether additional LA is required prior to re-dosing

**Entrustment question 6.** If you decide to re-do the epidural, what are the considerations in terms of level of insertion and managing the drugs for initiation and maintenance of the epidural block?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

The epidural can be re-attempted at a different space (usually a level higher), ideally by a second anaesthetist if the first insertion was particularly challenging, or if the initial anaesthetist feels stressed or tired.

- The patient should be closely monitored by the anaesthetic team due to the potential risk of intrathecal administration of drugs via the initial puncture site. High blocks when topping up for operative procedures are relatively common. Top-up cautiously with small aliquots and evaluate the block level with each top up - anticipate lower dose requirement.
- Always make sure that the dural puncture is carefully documented. Inform the patient, and the midwifery and anaesthetic team. One should exercise caution while injecting local anaesthetic via this newly placed catheter because of direct connection with the dural hole as well as seepage of a small amount of local anaesthetic via the dural hole.

**Entrustment question 7.** What are the strategies that you can adopt to minimize incidence of unintentional dural puncture?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- i. Identify and appreciate the ligamentum flavum during epidural needle advancement (although it might not be easy due to oedema in pregnant woman).
- ii. Understand the likely depth of the epidural space in an individual patient (ranges fr 4 - 5cm in most women) - can possibly be estimated using ultrasound with some training.
- iii. Advance the needle between contractions when unexpected patient movement is less likely.
- iv. Adequately control the needle-syringe assembly during advancement of the needle.
- v. Clear the needle of clotted blood or bone plugs.

**Entrustment question 8.** Are there any proven strategies to reduce occurrence of PDPH after accidental dural puncture?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

No intervention is very effective in preventing PDPH after unintentional dural puncture and the evidence supporting some measures is too weak to permit firm recommendations.

- Bed rest – no difference between bed rest and early ambulation
- Abdominal binder increases intra-abdominal and epidural pressure, which theoretically might help tamponade CSF loss, but is unsupported and has been abandoned because of the impracticalities
- Fluid intake - administering intravenous (IV) fluid or encouraging additional oral intake, with the hope of maximizing CSF production, does not prevent PDPH and makes management more difficult
- Intrathecal saline – not recommended due to inconclusive evidence
- Intrathecal insertion of an epidural catheter – mixed supporting evidence in reducing PDPH and EBP with intrathecal catheter placement
  - Deliberate subarachnoid placement of the epidural catheter at the time of dural puncture is a useful analgesic management option and several case series suggest it lowers the risk of PDPH after catheter removal
  - The rationale is that the presence of a catheter mechanically obstructs CSF loss when bearing down during childbirth and promotes an inflammatory response that aids puncture closure, although the latter supposition is based on an animal study involving weeks, not days, of catheterization
  - Initial opinions that the intrathecal catheter should be retained for more than 24 hours have not been supported by larger case series
  - An updated meta-analysis of nine studies found no significant reduction in PDPH but a significantly lower risk of EBP
  - A retrospective analysis of accidental dural punctures with 18 G needles, followed by intrathecal catheterization for at least 24 hours and post-delivery intrathecal saline infusion, found a reduction in PDPH from and a reduced need for EBPs
- Cerebral vasoconstrictors (caffeine and triptan) - a review concluded that caffeine is insufficiently supported to warrant prophylaxis against PDPH
  - Low-quality investigation of IV caffeine sodium benzoate 500 mg suggested PDPH after non-obstetric spinal anaesthesia was less severe
  - Multidose oral caffeine 75–150 mg combined with paracetamol (acetaminophen) 500 mg, started prior to spinal anaesthesia, and repeated regularly for 3 days after, is ineffective
  - A review concluded that caffeine is insufficiently supported to warrant prophylaxis against PDPH
- Neuraxial morphine – needs validation in larger trials
  - A small, randomized trial found no effect of subarachnoid morphine
  - A blinded study among parturient suffering unintentional puncture with a 17 G epidural needle reported a significantly reduced incidence of PDPH and EBP if epidural morphine 3 mg was given after delivery and again 24 hours postpartum
- Intravenous cosyntropin (tetracosactide) or dexamethasone – needs confirmation
  - A single, small, randomized trial in obstetric patients suffering an unintentional dural puncture found that IV cosyntropin 1 mg reduced the incidence of PDPH from 69% to 33% and the need for an EBP from 40% to 31%
  - Studies of IV dexamethasone are conflicting, with the largest randomized trial reporting that dexamethasone increased the incidence and severity of headache on the first postoperative day and had no effect thereafter
- Prophylactic epidural blood patch (PEBP)– no benefit and both a Cochrane systematic and a focused review had concluded that there was insufficient evidence to recommend the routine use of a PEBP
- Epidural saline or colloid - neither efficacy nor safety has been established

### Managing Postpartum Neurological Deficit

A previously healthy 38-year-old gravida 1, para 0 was admitted to labour and delivery in active labour at 38 + 5 weeks estimated gestational age. The patient received epidural anaesthesia for pain control. She progressed to complete dilation and effacement and began pushing efforts at +1 station. During the second stage of labour, different positioning techniques were utilized while pushing. No further descent of the foetal head was made over the course of four hours despite adequate contractions and active pushing, and she underwent an uncomplicated primary low transverse caesarean section for arrest of descent.

At 24 hours postoperatively, the patient complained of right-sided numbness over her lateral calf and the dorsum of her foot. She was able to stand and walk, but not without dragging her right foot and toes. An examination revealed preserved bilateral hip, knee, and ankle reflexes, with weak right-sided ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion, manifesting as a foot drop. The patient also reported difficulty passing urine, which required intermittent catheterization. Magnetic resonance imaging of the lumbar spine was obtained to evaluate for epidural hematoma, which showed no remarkable findings.

**Entrustment question 1.** What are the causes for postpartum neurological deficits?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

1. **Obstetric-related neuropathy** - lesion may be spinal, or the level of lumbosacral roots, lumbosacral trunks, or peripheral nerves.
   - Compressive :
     - Lumbosacral trunk by foetal head
     - Positioning - lithotomy, stirrups
     - Instrumental delivery
   - Ischaemic:
     - Prolonged hypotension
     - Obstruction of the internal iliac arteries by foetal head in a prolonged labour
2. **Anaesthetic-related** :
   - Trauma to spinal cord/nerve roots - direct needle or catheter injury, intraneural injection
   - Prolonged neural blockade - delayed recovery may be related to individual patient variation, total dose of LA used, physiological changes in pregnancy altering the pharmacokinetic of drugs
   - Bladder dysfunction - overstretching of bladder due to prolonged continuous epidural blockade, long-acting LA or intrathecal/epidural opioid administration
   - Cauda equine syndrome- incorrect neuraxial drug administration (intrathecally or via the epidural catheter) may result in devastating consequences such as CES due to neural toxicity
   - Transient neurological syndrome
   - Epidural hematoma - spontaneous, risk factors eg coagulopathy, bleeding disorders, difficult block placement
   - Epidural abscess or meningitis
   - Neurotoxicity or chemical arachnoiditis - CNB placement is a sterile procedure, and skin preparation involves the application of an alcohol-based sterile solution while taking care to avoid contaminating the needle and epidural catheter with this solution.
   - Vascular injury - Anterior spinal artery syndrome (anterior spinal cord infarction) may result from trauma, atherosclerosis, aortopathies, vasculitis, prolonged hypotension or emboli in the artery of Adamkiewicz.

The majority of neurological complications are due to compressive neuropathy as a result of prolonged labour or poor patient positioning or mode of delivery.
The temporal association between anaesthetic intervention with neuraxial block and onset of neurological symptoms often means that anaesthetists are consulted early in the presentation of a neurological complication following childbirth.
When neurological complications do occur, prompt recognition and management can reduce the risk of permanent neurological deficit.

**Entrustment question 2.** How would you evaluate the patient to obtain the likely cause?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

Despite the majority of nerve injury being related to factors other than regional anaesthesia/analgesia it is important to have knowledge of diagnosis, investigation and management of neurological injury.

A. Obtain full history:
   - Neurology with particular attention to pre-existing problems predisposing to neuropathy – backache, obesity, disc disease, malignancy, coagulopathy, infection, previous trauma.
   - Anaesthetic – review technique details if there has difficulties/paraesthesia at the time of spinal or epidural
   - Was there return of function after regional block before the development of symptoms?
   - Deteriorating symptoms or onset after a symptom - free interval should be treated seriously
   - Labour/Mode of delivery – instrumental delivery (type), posture during labour, use of retractors or diathermy, period of full dilatation, injections given by obstetrician, and hypotension
   - Drugs – particularly anti coagulants, steroids, hypoglycaemic

B. Examination – full neurological examination and examination of back
   - Delineate the extent of neurological deficit/s by performing a full neurological examination and careful documentation of deficit, including both sensory and motor signs by a senior anaesthetist
   - Refer neurology for a full neurological assessment

C. Investigation:
   - Consider MRI if there are red flags
   - Nerve conduction studies – done to
     1) differentiate between central and peripheral nerve injury
     2) identify the muscles affected
     3) prognosticate neuronal recovery
   - Serial temperature recordings, blood for WCC and C-reactive protein if infection suspected

**Entrustment question 3.** What is your management?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

1) If the neurological deficit is likely to be peripheral in origin:
   - Close observation and reassurance may be adequate as symptoms often resolve within 72h post delivery
     - Mild sensory deficit or symptom resolving – reassure patient and review in 4 weeks’ time
     - If symptoms persist with no improvement /worsening – consider neurology referral
   - If deficit is associated with motor weakness or progressively worsening :
     - Seek neurological +/- NES consultation to discuss for indication of nerve conduction studies or MRI
2) Urgent neurology opinion and MRI\* must be considered in all cases of suspected spinal injury, epidural, and spinal abscess or haematoma.
   - → \*Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography are used to confirm or exclude central neuraxial lesions such as an abscess, haematoma or tumour (magnetic resonance imaging is the preferred imaging modality, as it enables excellent visualisation of soft-tissue pathologies and inflammation, although computed tomography may be more easily accessible).
3) If nerve injury is suspected, nerve conduction studies and out-patient neurological referral at 6 weeks post-delivery is appropriate.
4) Many cases of nerve injury (obstetric and anaesthetic related) will become the subject of a complaint. Early and close liaison with a neurologist may be helpful.

**Entrustment question 4.** What are the red flags for postpartum neurological deficit?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

Look out for any “red flags” signs/symptoms:

- Progressive sensory dysfunction
- Flaccid paralysis below the injury
- Bowel and bladder dysfunction
- Axial pain at the level of puncture site/bleed
- Radicular irritation
- Late associated symptoms – fever/localised spinal pain/meningitic presentation

**Entrustment question 5.** 1 month latter you receive a letter from the patient stated she and husband are not satisfied and considering taking legal action. She had expressed her frustration trying to cope with her newborn with her ability to stand and walk properly at home. How would you respond to this complaint?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Always seek advice from Medical Malpractice Insurance before responding to a complaint, no matter how trivial it appears to be.
- Respond to all complaints promptly – either verbally or in writing.

Provision of a prompt response may lead to a quick resolution of the complaint and, as a result, less stress for the patient and you. A long delay in providing a response may result in the complainant’s view becoming entrenched, and a perception that the complaint is not being taken seriously, often making the complaint more difficult to resolve.

- If responding to a complaint verbally, ensure the discussion is documented.
- Ensure a professional tone – even if the complainant does not. A defensive or offensive response will only inflame the situation and may encourage the complainant to take the matter further.

The response is not the place to vent distress, frustration, or anger at the patient. The response is also not the place to criticise other doctors.

- Always carefully review a copy of the complaint and the relevant medical records before providing a response to a written complaint.

If other members of the hospital staff are involved in the complaint, ensure that they are provided with a copy of the complaint and their response is sought. You should not provide a response on behalf of other staff members.

- If the complaint is from a relative or other source, ensure that the patient’s confidentiality is not breached.

In these circumstances, a verbal or written authority from the patient should be obtained before providing a response to the third party.
In the response, express empathy for the patient’s concerns or disappointments. You should try to put yourself in the patient’s shoes and acknowledge the patient’s feelings. It is often useful to include in the response the words used by the patient in their letter of complaint, e.g. “I am sorry you are upset…”. This does not equate to an admission of liability, nor does it require the expression of guilt or wrongdoing.

- Address any misperceptions or inaccuracies expressed in the patient’s letter by summarising the events as you know them.

Be concise, but address each of the issues raised in the letter of complaint.
It may be useful to summarise the events and then use headings to address the allegations or complaints not already answered in the summary of the events.

### Managing Post Dural Puncture Headache

**Entrustment question 1.** What are the possible causes of a headache in this patient?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

Obstetric causes:

- Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
- Lactation headache/hormonal changes in oestrogen and progesterone levels
- Post-dural puncture headache
- Cerebral vein thrombosis, haematoma or infarct.

Non-obstetric causes:

- Common causes: dehydration, tension headache or migraine.
- Infective: meningitis/encephalitis/sinusitis
- Cerebrovascular: haemorrhage, haematoma, infarct or thrombosis
- Malignancy
- Illicit drug use
- Domestic violence

**Entrustment question 2.** What are the typical findings in the history and examination of a patient with a post-dural puncture headache?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

History:

- A dural puncture may have been identified at the time of the procedure (although in ~40% of PDPH following an epidural, the dural puncture was not recognised at the time of insertion).
- May be associated with multiple or difficult epidural insertion attempts, and with a low or high BMI.
- Commonly, the headache is fronto-occipital, worse on sitting or standing and improves when lying down (although in 5% of cases there is no postural element).
- Tinnitus, muffled, photophobia and neck stiffness hearing may be present.

Examination:

- Nerve palsies (most commonly VIth and VIIIth cranial nerves).
- In equivocal cases, circumferential squeezing of the abdominal may alleviate the headache (caval compression causes expansion of epidural venous circulation, which in term compresses the dural sac, producing an increase in CSF pressure and temporary alleviation of the headache).

Investigations:

- Observations and baseline blood tests are usually normal.

**Entrustment question 3.** What is the initial management of a patient with a PDPH?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

Conservative management should be attempted initially, to include:

- Adequate oral (or intravenous) hydration
- Simple analgesia: regular paracetamol and NSAID
- Antiemetics if required.
- Stool softeners to avoid straining.
- Encourage mobilisation; thromboembolic deterrent stockings and consideration of low-molecular-weight heparin prophylaxis if immobile.
- Regular reassessment, communication with woman and community midwifery team if discharged.
- Written instructions and discharge paperwork to accompany the woman.

**Entrustment question 4.** When and which further investigations will you consider in PDPH?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

The diagnosis of PDPH is made clinically by identifying the typical postural headache within 72 hours after a dural puncture. Other causes may need to be excluded if symptoms are atypical. Neuroimaging with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is not indicated unless required to exclude alternative diagnoses. If imaging is performed, findings consistent with PDPH include small ventricles, downward displacement (sagging) of the brain, engorged cerebral venous sinuses, subdural fluid collections, pituitary enlargement, and diffuse meningeal enhancement

*Embedded figure (UpToDate):* **Headache characteristics warranting prompt evaluation**

- Headache with altered mental status, seizures, papilledema, changes in vision, stiff neck, weakness, or focal neurological signs/symptoms
- Sudden onset of severe headache ("worst headache of my life")
- New-onset of migraine-type headache
- Headache in an immunosuppressed patient
- Change in headache characteristics (eg, pain, pattern, severity) from usual headaches
- Headache associated with/precipitated by fever, head trauma, nonprescription drug use, toxic exposure, cough, exertion, sexual activity, or Valsalva maneuver
- Headache that awakens the patient from sleep
- Headache unrelieved by pain medication

**Entrustment question 5.** How is an epidural blood patch performed?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

- Patient suitability: afebrile, no contraindication for epidural, more than 24 hours since puncture, and other causes for headache ruled out.
- Consent
  - Local bruising on the back.
  - Backache and stiffness, which can last a few days (no risk of long-term back pain).
  - Another accidental dural puncture.
  - Nerve damage, infection or bleeding.
  - Seizure at the time of performing the EBP.
  - Failure to treat headache: 30%–40%.
- Two anaesthetists, ideally one should be a consultant.
- Theatre environment.
- Full asepsis for both anaesthetists.
- Consultant locates epidural space at or below space at which the puncture occurred (because the injected blood is thought to spread in a cephalad direction).
- Other anaesthetist contemporaneously withdraws 30 ml blood.
- Blood injected via Tuohy until pain in back or all 30 ml used.
- Flush Tuohy with saline before withdrawal.
- Ensure the patient lies flat for two hours.
- Advise no lifting of weight heavier than her own baby for two weeks.
- Written discharge summary for GP, community midwife and woman to include issues that should prompt re-attendance at the hospital and contact details of the anaesthesia team.

\*Failure to treat headache: 30%–40%.

**Entrustment question 2.** What are the pathogenesis of pre-eclampsia and its associated complications?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

**Pathogenesis**:

- Pre-eclampsia has seen major advances in the past two decades, but its underlying pathogenesis is still not fully understood.
  - Pre-eclampsia can be understood in terms of both placental and maternal dysfunction.
  - Various genetic, angiogenic, structural, and metabolic pathways are implicated in pre-eclampsia, including spiral artery remodelling, placental oxygenation, redox and immune tolerance, and the balance of angiogenic and antiangiogenic factors.
  - Antiangiogenic proteins have emerged as key pathogenic mediators of pre-eclampsia, providing opportunities to develop novel diagnostics such as risk calculators, prediction models, and triage tools.

*Embedded figure (flow diagram):* Genetic factors (FLT1 SNPs and trisomy 13), Pre-existing risk factors (chronic hypertension, diabetes and antiphospholipid antibodies) and Immunological factors (dNK cells and T<sub>reg</sub> cell imbalance) → Shallow placentation → Stage I (first and second trimesters): Reduced placental perfusion (ER and oxidative stress) → Intrauterine growth restriction → Stage II (third trimester): ↑ Circulating sFLT1 and sENG; ↓ Circulating PlGF and VEGF; Other factors (e.g. obesity, pro-inflammatory factors and AT1 autoantibodies) → Systemic vascular dysfunction, capillary leak and vasospasm → (Proteinuria; Glomerular endotheliosis) / (Coagulation abnormalities (HELLP); Cerebral oedema (eclampsia)) / (Angiotensin II sensitivity; Hypertension).

*Fig. 1 | The pathogenesis of pre-eclampsia.* Genetic factors, maternal factors and immunological factors may cause placental dysfunction (stage I), which in turn leads to the release of antiangiogenic factors (such as soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1 (sFLT1) and soluble endoglin (sENG)) and other inflammatory mediators that induce pre-eclampsia (stage II). AT1, angiotensin II type I receptor; dNK, decidual natural killer; ER, endoplasmic reticulum; HELLP, haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelet count; PlGF, placental growth factor; SNP, single-nucleotide polymorphism; T<sub>reg</sub>, regulatory T cell; VEGF, vascular endothelial growth factor.

- **Diagnosis** based on the International Society of the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy (ISSHP) definition:
  - New onset BP (SBP >/= 140, DBP >/=90, or both)
  - After 20 weeks’ gestation
  - Accompanied by **one or more** features:
    - Proteinuria (> 1+, urine protein: creatinine ration >/= 30 mg mmol<sup>-1</sup>, albumin: creatinint ration >/= 8 mg mmol<sup>-1</sup>, or both)
    - Acute kidney injury (increase serum Cr >/= 90 µmol/L)
    - Neuro complications (seizure, severe headaches, persistent visual scotomata, clonus, blindness, altered mental status or stroke)
    - Haemato complications (thrombocytopenia plt < 150, DIVC, haemolysis)
    - Uteroplacental dysfunction (e.g. abnormal umbilical artery Doppler waveform analysis, restricted fetal growth or stillbirth)
  - Pre-eclampsia represents a potentially progressive clinical condition; therefore, the **sub-categories ‘mild’ and ‘severe’ should no longer be used**.
- **Potential complications**
  - Multi-systemic:
    - Neuro – Eclampsia, Stroke
    - CVS – Heart failure, Malignant HT
    - Respiratory – pulmonary oedema
    - Gastro – HELLP syndrome, pancreatitis
    - Renal – AKI
    - Haematological – Thrombocytopenia, DIVC
    - Foetal – Stillbirth, IUGR

Induction agents of choice:

- Propofol (pro: better laryngeal blunting than thio, con: potentially worse hypotension post-intubation than thio)
- Thiopentone (pro: wide use, con: hypotension with induction doses)

**Entrustment question 5.** What potential complications might you encounter, and how would you manage them?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

1) Difficult airway
   Early call for help
   Difficult airway algorithm for obstetrics

2) Peripartum cardiomyopathy or pre-eclampsia complicated by heart failure.

**Entrustment question 6.** What if the patient has severe pre-eclampsia with uncontrolled BP? How will this change your management and choice of anaesthesia?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

Proceeding with GA without stabilisation and uncertainty of heart function can be dangerous as GA doses of induction agents like thiopentone can have profound life-threatening hypotension.

Ideally a pre-operative TTE to elucidate cardiac function may be warranted if skills are available.

A low dose titrated RA technique (CSE) would also be preferable in the absence of contraindications or coagulopathy.

Otherwise, proceeding with close haemodynamic monitoring, slow titration of induction agents and intervention of BP swings are required for GA. Haemodynamic swings may persist throughout, and it will be prudent for close monitoring and fluid balance in the ICU post-operatively.

**Entrustment question 7.** Where should this patient go after delivery, and what should be monitored postoperatively?

**Entrustment discussion points:**

Monitored in ICU if:

- Need for respiratory support.
- Need for additional vasopressor or cardiovascular support.
- Need for invasive monitoring.
- Abnormal ECG requiring further intervention (e.g. cardioversion)
- Need for additional IV antihypertensive medications.
- Ace-base or severe electrolyte abnormalities needing correction.

What should be monitored:

- O2 saturations
- Ventilation
- HR
- Blood pressure (invasive arterial BP in high risk patients with resistant hypertension and heart failure)
- Accurate fluid balance
- Urine output
- Electrolyte, liver function and coagulation status
