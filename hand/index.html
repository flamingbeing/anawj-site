The 10 rules

Your senior will decide on surgery from your exam and your photos. If either is wrong, the patient gets the wrong plan. Read this box if nothing else.

  1. Examine sensation before any local anaesthetic (digital block, wound infiltration). Ask ED whether LA was already given. Numb from LA = exam useless.
  2. Test sensation distal to the wound, on the radial and ulnar side of the pulp separately. Testing proximal to the cut and writing "sensation intact" tells us nothing. Why ↓
  3. Test each tendon on its own: FDP and FDS for every finger at risk, FPL for the thumb, extensors at the MCP. "Able to make a fist" is not a tendon exam. How ↓
  4. Full movement does not mean the tendon is intact. A tendon can be 90% cut and still move. Pain or weakness against resistance = partial cut until proven otherwise. A wound over a tendon usually gets explored anyway.
  5. Look before you touch. The resting posture (the cascade) shows a cut flexor before you test anything, even in a child or a drunk patient.
  6. Bright pulsatile bleeding from the side of a finger = digital artery cut = assume the digital nerve is cut too (the nerve lies right next to it, just volar). Press and elevate; never clamp blindly, it crushes the nerve.
  7. Photos: always two views. One zoomed out (whole hand and wrist: which hand, which finger, which side) and one zoomed in (wound fills the frame). Wipe the blood off first. How ↓
  8. Use the right words: left/right, dominant hand, thumb / index / middle / ring / little, volar/dorsal, radial/ulnar side, and the level (DIP, middle phalanx, PIP…). Never "4th finger", "medial", "lateral". Words ↓
  9. Red swollen finger: check Kanavel's 4 signs. A wound over a knuckle from a punch is a fight bite until proven otherwise, however small. Infection ↓
  10. Take the rings off now (all fingers, before swelling). Glass injury → X-ray for glass. Document the time of injury.

Exam order (every hand, 5 minutes)

  1. History: age, dominant hand, job (and hobbies: musician? sport?), mechanism (knife / glass / saw / crush / bite / punch / high-pressure gun), time of injury, position of the hand when cut (fist or open), smoker, diabetes, tetanus, anticoagulants.
  2. Look: where exactly is the wound, the resting cascade, swelling, colour, deformity, rotation.
  3. Perfusion: colour, capillary refill vs the next finger, pulp turgor, temperature.
  4. Sensation (before LA): distal to the wound, radial and ulnar sides, light touch then 2PD.
  5. Tendons: each one on its own, then against resistance (pain? weak?).
  6. Bones and joints: tenderness, rotation (fist), stability; X-ray.
  7. Photos (wide + close, plus extras for the picture), then call with a structured message.

By clinical picture

🔪 Cut / laceration (finger, palm, wrist)

Examine

  • Exact site: digit, volar/dorsal, radial/ulnar side, level (e.g. "volar, radial side, over proximal phalanx" = flexor zone II).
  • Cascade at rest. A finger lying straighter than its neighbours = flexor cut.
  • Perfusion of the digit (cap refill, colour, turgor) vs neighbours.
  • Sensation distal to the cut: radial and ulnar pulp separately, light touch + 2PD, compare to the same finger on the other hand.
  • FDP and FDS of every finger near the cut, FPL if thumb/thenar. Extensors at MCP if dorsal. Then against resistance: pain? weak?
  • Glass: X-ray (most glass > 2 mm shows).
  • Volar wrist cut ("spaghetti wrist"): test median (thumb abduction, index pulp), ulnar (finger spreading, little pulp), radial and ulnar pulses, every finger flexor and wrist flexor (FCR, FCU, PL).

Photos

  • Wide: whole hand + wrist, the side with the wound.
  • Close-up: wound fills half the frame, after cleaning.
  • Cascade: side view, hand relaxed palm up.
  • Short video of each at-risk finger: FDP test, FDS test.

Call now if

  • Pale / cold / no cap refill digit.
  • Uncontrolled bleeding despite 10 min pressure + elevation.
  • Multiple tendons/nerves at the wrist.

Common miss: testing sensation over the palm for a finger cut; "flexes all fingers" without isolating FDS; not noticing that the patient was gripping (knife or glass) when cut, so the tendon end has retracted away from the skin wound.

🔥 Red, swollen finger or hand (infection)

Examine

  • Kanavel's 4 signs (flexor sheath infection): held flexed, sausage swelling, tender along the sheath, pain on passive extension. Details ↓
  • Where is the pus? Nail fold (paronychia), pulp (felon), sheath, web space, palm, dorsum, joint.
  • Any puncture, splinter, bite, injection, recent manicure? Diabetes, immunosuppression, IV drug use.
  • Mark the edge of the redness with a pen, write date and time on the skin, photograph it.
  • Temperature, HR, BP; WCC, CRP, glucose; X-ray (foreign body, gas, bone).

Photos

  • Wide: both hands side by side, palms up (swelling is obvious only when compared).
  • Close-up of the finger: volar and side view.
  • Dorsal view (palm infections swell the back of the hand).
  • After pen marking, with the time written.

Call now if

  • Any Kanavel sign, or a swollen whole finger.
  • Pain out of proportion, rapid spread, dusky skin, blisters, crepitus, unwell: think necrotising fasciitis.
  • Sepsis.

Common miss: calling it "cellulitis" without checking passive extension and sheath tenderness; treating dorsal swelling as the problem when the pus is in the palm.

🦷 Bite, or punch with a wound over the knuckle

Examine

  • Fight bite: small wound over an MCP (often ring/little, dominant hand) after punching someone in the mouth. Patients often deny it. Assume the tooth went into the joint.
  • Look at the wound with the fist clenched as well as open: in a fist the tendon and joint capsule sit under the skin wound; when the finger straightens the tendon cut slides proximal and hides.
  • Extensor (MCP extension) of that finger, against resistance.
  • Joint: swelling, pain on moving the MCP.
  • X-ray: tooth fragment, metacarpal head fracture ("boxer's" neck fracture is common too).
  • Animal bites (cat especially): small deep punctures, high infection risk; check for sheath signs.

Photos

  • Wide: dorsum of the hand, fingers straight.
  • Close-up: wound.
  • Same close-up with the fist clenched.

Call now if

  • Every fight bite: usually needs washout in theatre.
  • Any Kanavel sign after a bite.

Common miss: a 3 mm "abrasion" over the knuckle sent home with oral antibiotics, back in 3 days with septic arthritis.

🔨 Crush, suspected fracture or dislocation

Examine

  • Rotation: ask for a loose fist. All fingertips should point towards the scaphoid (base of the thumb) and not overlap. A finger crossing over its neighbour (scissoring) = rotated fracture, needs fixing. If pain stops a fist, look end-on at the nails with fingers half bent: nail planes should line up. Compare with the other hand.
  • Swelling, deformity, open wound over a fracture (= open fracture: antibiotics, tetanus per protocol).
  • Perfusion and sensation distal to the injury.
  • Tendons: can the patient flex and extend each joint? (A mallet finger is a tendon or bony avulsion: DIP droops, can't straighten actively.)
  • X-ray: PA and true lateral of the injured finger (not just "hand"), oblique for metacarpals.

Photos

  • Wide: hand, palm down, fingers straight.
  • Loose fist from the front (rotation).
  • End-on view of the fingertips (nail alignment).
  • X-rays: photo of the screen, straight on, whole image, one per view.

Call now if

  • Unreduced dislocation, open fracture, ischaemic digit, compartment syndrome signs (tense, pain on passive stretch).

Common miss: rotation not checked; X-ray of the "hand" where the finger lateral is overlapped by the other fingers.

💅 Fingertip, nailbed injury or amputation

Examine

  • Level of tissue loss: pulp only? bone exposed? through nail? Which way is the loss angled (more volar or more dorsal)?
  • Nail: avulsed, lifted out of the fold, subungual haematoma size; nailbed laceration with a tuft fracture = open fracture.
  • Amputation: level, clean cut vs crush/avulsion, condition of the part, time of injury, smoker, job, dominance, patient's wishes.
  • Amputated part: wrap in saline-moist gauze → sealed bag → bag in ice-water. Never put it directly on ice or in water.

Photos

  • Wide: whole hand, palm and back.
  • Close-up of the stump: from the front (volar), the side and end-on.
  • Amputated part: both sides, next to a ruler.
  • X-ray of the hand and of the part.

Call now if

  • Any amputation proximal to the nail fold, thumb amputation, multiple digits, any child: possible replant, time matters.
💉 High-pressure injection (paint, grease, hydraulic, fuel)

Examine

  • Tiny entry wound, often on the index fingertip or palm, looks harmless. Patient may have little pain at first.
  • What substance, what pressure, time. Swelling tracking up the finger/palm, perfusion, sensation.
  • X-ray (lead paint and air show the spread).
  • Elevate. No digital block (it adds pressure in the finger).

Photos

  • Wide + close-up of the entry point; whole limb if swelling spreads.

Call now

  • Always. Surgical emergency: needs urgent debridement; amputation risk rises by the hour.
🩸 Pale, cold or blue finger

Examine (compare with the next finger)

  • Colour: white = no arterial inflow; blue/purple and swollen = venous congestion.
  • Capillary refill: press the pulp or nailbed, release, count. Normal < 2 s, same as neighbours. Instant refill in a dusky finger = congested.
  • Pulp turgor: flat and soft = arterial; tense = venous.
  • Temperature vs neighbours.
  • Doppler both digital arteries if available. A finger survives on one digital artery, so pink does not mean both are intact.

Photos

  • Wide: the hand with neighbouring fingers for colour comparison, in normal light, no flash (flash changes colour).
  • Close-up of the digit.

Call now

  • Always. Revascularisation is time-critical.

Sensation

1. Where to test: distal to the wound, each side separately

Each finger has two digital nerves: radial digital nerve on the radial side, ulnar digital nerve on the ulnar side, running along the volar-lateral edge next to the digital artery. Each supplies its own half of the finger distal to where it runs. So:

  • Test the pulp (fingertip) on the radial half and the ulnar half separately, for every finger distal to the wound. Use the side of the tip, not the middle.
  • Skin proximal to the cut is supplied by nerve that hasn't been cut yet. It will always feel normal. Writing "sensation intact" from there is false reassurance.
  • A cut in the palm can divide a common digital nerve, which splits into the facing sides of two fingers. So a palm cut between middle and ring can numb the ulnar side of the middle finger and the radial side of the ring finger. Test both fingers either side of a palm wound.

Pulsatile bleed from the side of a finger? The digital artery is cut. The nerve sits just volar to it, so it is very likely cut too, even if the patient says it feels "a bit normal". Say so to your senior.

2. Light touch: always a comparison

  1. Patient's hand resting palm up on a pillow or table. Patient looks away.
  2. Touch the normal side (e.g. the uninjured ulnar pulp, or the same finger on the other hand) lightly with your fingertip or cotton wool, then the side at risk.
  3. Ask: "Does this feel the same as this, or different?" Not "can you feel this?" (patients feel pressure even with a cut nerve and will say yes).
  4. If different: "Numb, reduced, or tingly? If normal is 10 out of 10, what is this?" (the Ten test: quick, works in children from about 5 years).

"Altered" or "a bit numb" on one side of the pulp after a cut on that side = nerve cut until proven otherwise. Partial nerve cuts often feel "a bit different", not numb.

3. Two-point discrimination (2PD): the exact method

The most objective bedside test. Do it properly or don't write a number.

Tool

  • 2PD disc (Disk-Criminator) or calipers if your unit has them.
  • Otherwise a paperclip: bend open into a U, set the two ends at the distance you want with a ruler, check both tips are level (touch at the same time) and blunt. Make two: one at 5 mm, one at 10 mm. One single straight end is your "one point".

Position

  • Hand supported, palm up, fingers still (rest the back of the finger on the table or your hand). A moving finger gives moving 2PD, which is a different test.
  • Demonstrate on a normal finger first with eyes open: "This is one. This is two."
  • Then eyes closed or looking away.

Technique

  1. Test the pulp, distal to the DIP crease, on the radial half, then the ulnar half.
  2. Points in line with the length of the finger (longitudinal), both on one half of the pulp. Never across the midline: then each point lands on a different nerve and even a cut nerve "passes".
  3. Light pressure: just until the skin blanches, not digging in. Both points touch at the same moment. Hold ~2 s, then ask "one or two?"
  4. Randomly mix one point and two points. Don't always start with two. Patients learn the pattern.
  5. Start at 5 mm. Correct in at least 7 of 10 (or 2 of 3 if short of time) = passes at that distance. If not, go wider (8, 10, 15 mm) until they pass.
  6. Record the smallest distance passed, for each side, and the same side on the uninjured finger.

Compare, don't just quote numbers. A labourer with thick calluses may be 8 mm everywhere. What matters: this side vs the other side of the same finger, vs the same finger on the other hand. Write it like this: "Left index: 2PD radial pulp 12 mm, ulnar pulp 4 mm (right index 4/4 mm)."

4. Children, unconscious or uncooperative patients

  • Wrinkle test: soak the hand in warm water for 10–30 min. Denervated skin does not wrinkle. Photograph the wrinkled vs smooth pulp.
  • Dry skin: denervated skin stops sweating; a plastic pen glides over it instead of dragging.
  • Watch what the child avoids using. Ten test from about 5 years.
  • If you cannot assess, say "unable to assess", not "intact".

6. Pitfalls that make your sensation exam worthless

  • Testing after local anaesthetic, or after a ring block "to clean the wound".
  • Testing proximal to the wound, or on the middle of the pulp instead of each side.
  • Asking "can you feel this?" instead of "same or different?".
  • 2PD points across the finger, pressing hard enough to dent, always starting with two, patient watching.
  • Not comparing with the other side and the other hand.
  • Writing "NV intact" for a whole hand. Write each digit and side.

Tendons: test each one on its own

1. Look first: the cascade and tenodesis

With the hand relaxed, palm up, wrist neutral, the fingers rest progressively more bent from index to little: the cascade. A finger lying straighter than it should has lost its flexor pull.

  • Tenodesis test (works in children and patients who won't cooperate): gently extend the patient's wrist → the fingers curl into flexion; flex the wrist → the fingers straighten. A finger that doesn't curl with wrist extension = flexor cut. A finger that doesn't straighten with wrist flexion = extensor cut.
  • Photograph the cascade (side view). It is often the single most useful picture for your senior.

2. Flexors

FDP (bends the fingertip, DIP)

  1. Hold the patient's middle phalanx firmly, PIP straight.
  2. "Bend just the tip of your finger."
  3. DIP bends = FDP working. Then press back against the tip: pain? weak vs other hand?

Do every finger, not just the cut one.

FDS (bends the middle joint, PIP)

  1. Hold all the other fingers fully straight (flat on the table). This blocks FDP, which shares one muscle belly for middle, ring and little.
  2. "Bend this finger."
  3. Bends at the PIP with a floppy DIP (you can wiggle the tip) = FDS working. If the DIP bends too, you didn't hold the others straight enough.

Index: its FDP is independent, so this test can mislead. Ask for a firm pulp-to-pulp pinch with the thumb: FDS working = pinch with the DIP straight/hyperextended; tip-to-tip pinch with the DIP bent = relying on FDP. Little: FDS is weak or absent in a fair number of people. Compare with the other hand before calling it cut.

FPL (bends the thumb tip)

Hold the thumb's proximal phalanx still, "bend the tip of your thumb". IP flexes = FPL working. Test against resistance.

3. Partial cuts and where the tendon end went

  • Full movement ≠ intact. Up to 90% of a tendon can be cut with full active movement. Signs of a partial cut: pain on movement or against resistance, weakness vs the other hand, catching/triggering. Write "FDP flexes fully but painful and weak against resistance", not "intact".
  • Fist at the time of injury (gripping a knife or a breaking glass): the tendon was cut with the finger bent, so when the finger straightens the cut sits distal to the skin wound, hidden. Ask how the hand was positioned.
  • A wound over the line of a tendon in a zone where the tendon lies right under the skin (fingers, palm, wrist) is usually explored regardless. Your job is to report accurately, not to rule it out.

5. Zones (say the zone to your senior)

Extensor zones: odd numbers over joints: I DIP, II middle phalanx, III PIP, IV proximal phalanx, V MCP, VI metacarpal, VII wrist (under the retinaculum), VIII distal forearm. Thumb: T-I IP, T-II proximal phalanx, T-III MCP, T-IV metacarpal.

6. Document like this

Right middle finger: FDP flexes DIP fully, painless against resistance. FDS flexes PIP with floppy DIP, painful and weak against resistance vs left. Index and ring FDP/FDS normal. Cascade: middle slightly straighter than ring. Video sent.

Send short videos (5–10 s) of the FDP and FDS test for each finger at risk, and the patient making a fist and opening it. A video of movement tells your senior more than any photo.

Infection: Kanavel's signs and the look-alikes

Kanavel's 4 signs: flexor sheath infection

  1. Finger held slightly bent at rest (most comfortable for a swollen sheath).
  2. Fusiform (sausage) swelling of the whole finger, not just one spot.
  3. Tender along the flexor sheath on the volar side, from fingertip to the distal palm (press along the line of the tendon; compare with the sides of the finger).
  4. Pain on passive extension: gently straighten the finger yourself. Often the earliest and most telling.

Early on not all 4 are present. Even one or two, with a swollen finger: call your senior. It can destroy the tendon within a day and usually needs theatre washout. Start treatment per unit protocol after cultures; elevate.

Thumb and little finger sheaths connect to the radial and ulnar bursae at the wrist: infection can spread from one to the other (horseshoe abscess). Swelling and tenderness over the wrist or thenar/hypothenar = more urgent.

Photos your senior can use

Your senior is deciding from a phone screen whether to come in, book theatre or send home. A close-up of a red blob is unusable.

Always 2 views, minimum:
  1. Wide (context): whole hand and wrist in the frame, fingers spread. Shows which hand, which finger, palm or back, and the level of the wound.
  2. Close-up: the wound fills about half the frame, sharp, edges visible.

Before you press the button

  1. Clean: wipe off blood and dried clot with saline gauze; press to stop oozing for a moment. Remove dressings fully.
  2. Background: plain blue or green drape / towel. Not bloody sheets, not the patient's lap, no clutter.
  3. Light: good room light or daylight; avoid shadows from your own head. Flash makes wet wounds glare and changes colour; if you use it, check the result.
  4. Square-on: phone parallel to the skin, straight above it, not at an angle (angles distort size and position).
  5. Focus: tap the wound on the screen. Move the phone closer instead of digital zoom.
  6. Scale: a paper ruler or tape measure next to the wound for the close-up.
  7. Check it: zoom into your own photo. If you can't tell what it shows, neither can your senior.
